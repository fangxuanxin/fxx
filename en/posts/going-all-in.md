# Is Going All-In a Kind of Wisdom?

> Published: 2026-11-10 · Tags: Notes
> Author: FXX · Web version: https://fxx.me/en/posts/going-all-in/

Going all-in is a kind of wisdom, and Chinese people ought to go all-in.

Scrolling on my phone over the past couple of years, I seem to run into this phrase and this argument all the time. The videos usually come with a grand, sweeping theory, starting from thousands of years of living at the mercy of the weather and surviving wars, and then rolling out a string of examples: someone bet everything on Bitcoin and became Justin Sun, or the founder of Huolala went all-in back in the day and built Huolala into what it is today.

Yet when you look away from the screen and turn back to yourself and the people around you in everyday life, especially right now, hardly anyone talks about going all-in, and even fewer actually do it. What friends and family talk about day to day is mostly how to budget carefully, and how to live the life in front of them steadily and well.

I kept wondering why the rhetoric online and the life around us feel so far apart, and I think it comes down to the gambler's mentality inside human nature. If you talk about going all-in, very few people around you would actually push all their chips to the middle of the table, though people are not just watching that rhetoric for a laugh either. If you talk about gambling in general, however, without even getting into percentages, almost everyone knows a few people who like playing cards, betting a little money, or picking up a lottery ticket on the way home. Once you see that, the rest makes sense: all those claims online that "Chinese people ought to go all-in" are simply another ordinary desire magnified by the internet.

When someone online says what you would never dare say out loud, you give them a like; when a fleeting thought that crossed your mind for a second is spoken aloud on a screen, you feel as if you have found a kindred spirit. The talk of going all-in does much the same thing, taking a small trace of greed inside each individual and magnifying it. Dressed up with famous names, ready-made examples, and a bold label, it gives viewers a rush. In that version of the story, the outcome no longer seems to have anything to do with personal ability or years of effort; it comes down entirely to whether you dare to place the bet, which happens to be the very thing most of us only dare to think about and never do. It feels thrilling to listen to, even if very few people in real life ever act on it.

Looked at the other way around, this also shows something about how expression works online: if you want attention, you have to turn up the volume. Imagine two people making videos, one saying it is enough to buy a lottery ticket now and then for fun, and the other telling you to go straight all-in because they do not have to bear the consequences through the screen. It is not hard to guess which one will stimulate your nerves and get the views. Watch enough of those videos, though, and you find you no longer have the patience for ordinary, quiet things. That is one of the downsides of how the internet amplifies the senses: everything is pushed to extremes. In short dramas, people kneel and beg for forgiveness at the slightest disagreement and act with ruthless decisiveness the next second; in short videos, everything is black or white, all-in or dead. It gets your blood boiling, while in reality you have not even put your phone down. Minds are shaped, little by little, by that constant stimulation.

There is an old saying: when water is full, it overflows; when the moon is full, it begins to wane. Tradition tells us not to speak in absolutes, which works fine when you are talking to one or two people. Once your audience reaches tens of thousands, though, for anyone trying to stand out online and make a name for themselves, every statement has to be pushed all the way to the edge and beyond. Over the years everyone has learned that getting attention means chasing eyeballs, a global formula that asks you to throw caution to the wind, stop strangers on the street for interviews, and clown around for the camera.

Seen from that angle, the videos preaching about going all-in are actually fairly tame. After all, the people on screen are only moving their lips; they are not really pushing their own chips in. The people who truly practiced going all-in in real life have either gone to zero already, or sewn their mouths shut.

Everyone left talking and watching is probably just in it for the entertainment.
